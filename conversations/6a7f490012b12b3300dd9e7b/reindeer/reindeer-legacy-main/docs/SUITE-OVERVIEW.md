# My Reindeer Legacy

### An overview for owners, families, and trustees

## Why this exists

Estates rarely come apart over money. They come apart over the dining table.

Cash divides cleanly. What does not is the grandfather clock, the wedding china, the tools in the
garage. A will usually disposes of all of it in one sentence — "my tangible personal property to be
divided among my children as they shall agree" — and that sentence is where the trouble starts. It
asks a grieving family to agree, without saying how, at the worst moment of their lives.

## Two perspectives, one estate

The suite is built around one honest observation: **the same estate looks completely different
depending on who is standing in front of it, and when.**

**While the owner is alive, the estate is theirs.** They know which objects have a story, which
ones they always meant for someone in particular, and which ones are just furniture. Registry's
only job in this window is to serve *their* wishes — to make it easy to say, on the record and in
their own words, *this goes to her, and here is why.* Nobody else can do this work. Nobody else
should try.

**After the owner is gone, the estate belongs to the heirs.** The named gifts are honored; that
part is settled by what the owner wrote down. What was not specifically directed — the tangible
leftovers, which is most of the house — now has to be divided among people who did not choose to
be doing this and do not agree on what things are worth. FairPlay is the app the heirs use to
do that work together. They run it. The trustee named by the trust or will has, in most estates,
already delegated the personal-property division to them.

**The trustee sits outside the app by default — but can step inside if it helps.** *Trustee* is
the suite's word for the person named by the trust or will to wind up the estate. Your legal
documents may name that same person *executor*, *personal representative*, or *administrator*
depending on state and instrument; in this app they are always **trustee**. In most families they
delegate the personal-property division to the heirs and stay out of the room; what they retain,
and cannot delegate, is responsibility for the **high-value items** and the **financial-balance-sheet
fairness** of the estate. FairPlay segregates the high-value bucket for them: those items come
out of the heir draft and go to the trustee's formal appraisal, where they are equalized against
other trust assets. Everything else stays in the family draft.

When it would help — a blended family, an heir who cannot fairly run the session, a sensitive
estate — the trustee can also sign in and run the session as the **captain** (the person moving
the game forward). They still never draft or receive items; running the game and receiving items
are separate things. The captain hat is a role, not a category: an heir can wear it, or a trustee
can wear it. The heirs may also invite a **representative** — a neutral third party (a family
friend, a mediator, an heir's chosen delegate, a trustee's delegate) — and ask that person to
wear the captain hat instead. The representative doesn't draft or receive either.

### Who can be captain

Because the app cannot compel anyone to use it, the choice of captain is the heirs' to make.
The captain is whoever the heirs will accept running the game — otherwise buy-in collapses and
the result has no weight. That said, only four kinds of person are allowed to wear the captain
hat:

1. **An heir** — the common case. One of the beneficiaries runs the game for the group.
2. **The trustee** — when the heirs want the person with legal responsibility to also referee.
3. **A trustee's representative** — someone the trustee has designated to act on their behalf
   inside the app (an attorney, a family-office staffer, an accountant). Marked as a
   representative on the roster and tied to the trustee they represent.
4. **An heir's representative** — someone the heirs have collectively picked to run the session
   for them (a neutral family friend, a mediator, a professional). Marked as a representative on
   the roster and tied to the heir group.

The two rules that make the vocabulary permanent still hold:

- **An heir is never a trustee.** An heir may run the session as captain, but never carries the
  legal duty to the estate; that duty belongs to the trustee named by the instrument.
- **A trustee is never an heir.** A trustee may run the session as captain, but never drafts,
  ranks, receives items, or otherwise participates as a beneficiary.

And two rules govern representatives:

- **A representative never receives items** and never enters the distribution math. They wear
  the captain hat and nothing else.
- **A representative is always tied to the person or side they represent**, and that link is
  visible on the roster. Nobody is anonymously in charge.

All of these configurations use the same vocabulary: **heir**, **trustee**, **representative**,
and **captain** (the role any of them can wear while running the session). There is no boss
inside the app — only a referee.

### Method agreement — the buy-in

Before the game does anything with real consequences, every heir signs a plain-language
**method agreement** that names the current captain and states, on the record, that the heirs
accept this person running the process. If the heirs replace the captain mid-way — because
the heir who volunteered can no longer be neutral, because the trustee steps in, because
everyone agrees a representative would be better — every heir signs the agreement again,
naming the new captain. Prior signatures don't roll forward silently. Buy-in is the whole basis
for the result being worth anything, so it is asked for out loud, every time it changes.

### Graceful exit

At any point in the process, any signed-in participant can save a **snapshot** — a printable
record of everything the app knows right now (roster, method-agreement signatures across every
captain era, items, rankings, audit log). It's explicitly not a decision and not the Record of
Decisions. It's what the heirs hand to the trustee if they stop using the app for any reason:
the trustee then handles the distribution outside the software under the will or trust. Nobody
is trapped inside the app.

So the suite is two applications, sold separately, because two different people are doing two
different jobs at two different moments — and a third person (the trustee) is watching over the
top of both. Usually the trustee stays outside the apps; when the family needs it, the trustee
can step inside FairPlay as captain.

| | **Reindeer: Registry** | **Reindeer: FairPlay** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who uses it | The **owner**, while living | The **heirs**, together, after |
| Whose desires it serves | The owner's | The heirs', within the trust or will |
| The trustee's role | Recipient of the signed schedule | Handles high-value items and financial balance from outside the app |
| The job | Write down what exists and what was meant for whom | Divide what remains, without a fight |
| Produces | A signed schedule for the trustee | A Record of Decisions the trustee countersigns |
| Needs the other? | No | No |

Either is useful alone. Together, the first feeds the second, and both hand the trustee something
they can actually work with.

## Three protections

The suite is designed to protect three parties at once. Each of the three has a distinct interest
that the software is built to defend:

**Protecting the estate.** Items likely to be valuable are flagged by an AI value estimate at
intake and again at ranking, and they are segregated into a high-value bucket that the family
draft never touches. This is how the software prevents the most common personal-property
mistake: a materially valuable item quietly divided as if it were bric-a-brac, before anyone had
it appraised. Nothing significant leaves the estate without the trustee seeing it.

**Protecting the heirs.** The rules of the family draft are agreed to in writing before any heir
knows who will get what. Rankings are private. The process is recorded step by step. At the end
every heir signs the Record of Decisions, and no heir can be told later that they consented to
something they never saw. This is what turns a family conversation that would otherwise erode
for a generation into a decision the family can live with.

**Protecting the trustee.** The high-value flag is not only an estate-protection feature; it is
also a trustee-protection feature. A trustee who signs off on a distribution where a materially
valuable item was informally handed out is exposed. FairPlay pulls those items into the
trustee's queue automatically, so the trustee does not have to trust the family to have
recognized them. The Record of Decisions then gives the trustee a clean, signable audit trail
for the trust file: who agreed to what rules, in what order the items were assigned, and who
signed off at the end.

## Two failures the suite is built to prevent

**Nobody knows what there is.** By the time a trustee arrives, the contents of a house are
undocumented. What the owner knew — that the plain-looking bowl was their mother's, that
the rifle in the closet came home from France with a grandfather — died with them. Things are lost,
given away twice, or sold for nothing because nobody recognized them. Registry closes this gap
*while the owner is still able to close it.*

**There is no fair way to divide what remains.** Three children and one clock is not a problem
arithmetic solves. Families default to birth order, to whoever asks first, or to whoever is willing
to fight, and the resentment outlasts everyone involved. FairPlay runs a defined, recorded
process the heirs agree to in advance, with the high-value items pulled out for the trustee to
handle formally. The remainder is theirs to divide, and the Record of Decisions is what the
trustee signs at the end.

## Reindeer: Registry

The owner walks through their own house with a phone and photographs things. That is the whole of
it.

The decision underneath everything else is that **the room, not the item, is the unit of work.** A
list of two hundred objects is a task nobody starts. A kitchen is a task somebody finishes on a
Tuesday afternoon. The app tracks which rooms have been walked, so an inventory built over weeks
with interruptions still knows where the owner left off. Photographs are named automatically, and
the owner can speak about an item rather than type — the story of an object is usually the only
part that cannot be reconstructed later, and it is the part a keyboard discourages.

**Nothing is required.** An item saves with no room, no value, no category, no recipient. This is
the most important rule in the application. Every mandatory field is a place where somebody stops,
and an incomplete inventory of two hundred items is worth far more than a perfect inventory of
nine.

**On naming who gets what.** Registry lets an owner record that a specific item is intended for a
specific person. It does not ask. There is no prompt, no empty field styled as unfinished business,
no badge marking an item undecided — because the moment the app implies a choice is expected, the
owner stops documenting and starts deciding, and deciding is slow. Most items should carry no name
at all. The named ones are the exceptions the owner already had in mind.

**Seven printed documents** come out of it, all on ordinary paper: a list by room; a list by
category; a list by intended recipient; a high-value list for the attorney or appraiser; an
individual page for a single object and its story; a memorandum for signature; and a trustee packet
assembling the lot. Values stay hidden unless deliberately shown, so a list can go to the family
without handing over an appraisal.

**The memorandum.** Most US states allow a will to refer to a separate written list disposing of
tangible personal property, which the owner may then revise without re-executing the will.
Registry prints that list for signature. **Schedule A** carries the items with a named recipient
and is the operative page; **Schedule B** lists everything else — property that is documented but
which the memorandum does not direct.

This is not a will and not legal advice, and every printed copy says so on its face. Whether a
memorandum is effective depends on the state and on the wording of the will it refers to. It must
go to the drafting attorney. The document is built to arrive on that desk already organized, which
is a more modest claim than being valid.

## Reindeer: FairPlay

Where Registry documents, FairPlay divides — the heirs run it together, and the trustee stays
outside handling the high-value items.

One heir sets up the estate in the app, adds the other heirs, and loads the inventory. From
there everyone drafts. Each heir ranks the items they care about, privately, without seeing
anyone else's choices. The application resolves those rankings into an allocation and handles
the items more than one person wants. Any value balancing between shares belongs to the
trustee's workflow outside the app, informed by the Record of Decisions and any appraisal
flags on it. When the session ends, every heir signs the Record of Decisions and the trustee
countersigns it — that is the document that closes the tangible-property portion of the
estate.

**High-value items get flagged for the trustee.** FairPlay flags items whose AI value
estimate crosses the estate's threshold and puts them in the trustee's queue on the Record
of Decisions. Formal appraisal and any value balancing against other trust assets happen in
the trustee's own workflow outside the app, on their usual timeline. This is the specific
piece the software prevents families from doing informally, because it is the piece where a
mistake puts the trustee in legal jeopardy.

Three things make the heir-run half work where a family conversation does not. **Preferences
stay private until resolved**, so nobody anchors on a sibling's list and nobody has to say out
loud that they want their mother's ring. **The process is identical for everyone**, so there
is no advantage in asking first. **Every step is recorded.**

FairPlay also ships a **practice mode** — the full lifecycle on sample data with no real
awards. The heirs can walk through it together once before anything is at stake. Families who
have already seen the screens argue considerably less about the screens.

FairPlay is the comprehensive half of the suite: fifteen rooms and twenty-five categories
built around distinctions the law actually draws — firearms, precious metals, coins and stamps,
vehicles, collectibles. Registry is deliberately the plainer half.

## The handoff — owner's record to the family's draft

The seam between the two apps is the seam between the two perspectives. Registry is the owner
finishing sentences that only they can finish. FairPlay, from the first login, is the heirs
running a fair process on what the owner did not specifically direct, with the high-value bucket
carved out for the trustee. Nothing in the owner's record is silently reinterpreted at that
boundary — the heirs see exactly what arrived, and accept it deliberately.

**What carries across:** every item, its room and category, its photographs, its recorded value,
and any intended recipient the owner named — which arrives as a *note*, never a binding award, so
the heirs (and, for high-value items, the trustee) can honor it against the will or trust rather
than the software substituting for that judgement. Each item keeps a trail back to the Registry
record it came from. Room and category names FairPlay has not seen are flagged for approval;
it does not silently invent structure in an estate it has just been handed.

**What does not:** the people. Registry knows the owner's family; FairPlay needs its heirs
registered on its own terms with their own sign-ins. The trustee's name is captured (optionally)
at setup and prints on the Record of Decisions. In the common case the trustee stays outside the
app and does not log in; when the heirs choose the trustee (or a trustee's representative) as
captain, that person signs in and runs the session (see "Who can be captain" above). This
retyping is a real gap either way.

## What a trustee receives

A schedule of tangible personal property, itemized, photographed, and organized room by room. A
signed memorandum identifying the items intended for specific people. A high-value list flagging
property likely to need appraisal. All of it on paper, and the underlying data as an open export —
not locked inside an app.

That organization matches how the IRS expects tangible property to be reported. Federal regulation
asks for estate property to be itemized **room by room**, and permits grouping within one room
where no item exceeds $100
([26 CFR 20.2031‑6(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/20.2031-6)). Any article of artistic
or intrinsic value **above $3,000** requires appraisal by an expert, submitted under oath
([26 CFR 20.2031‑6(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/20.2031-6)). Registry's high-value
list exists to find those articles before the estate is under a deadline. Reporting requirements
are in the [Form 706 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i706).

## What this is not

Not a will, not a codicil, not a substitute for an attorney. Recorded values are the owner's own
estimate unless a real appraisal is entered, and are labeled as such — this is not a tax
valuation. It does not make wishes legally binding; that takes a lawyer and an executed document.
It will not resolve a dispute a family is determined to have.

What it does is make sure that when the family sits down, they are looking at the same list.

## Who this was built for

The person doing the hardest work is often in their seventies or eighties, using a phone they did
not choose, on an afternoon they would rather spend otherwise.

So: large type, large targets, plain words, a confirmation before anything irreversible, nothing
mandatory, and every screen printable — because a document that exists only on a screen does not
feel, to that generation, like a document that exists. Those are constraints, not preferences. An
estate application that is unpleasant to use produces an empty database.
