# Reindeer: Registry

Part of **My Reindeer Legacy**. Sold separately from Reindeer: FairPlay.

---

## Whose app this is

Registry is the **owner's** app, used **while the owner is alive.** Every screen answers to *their*
desires and *their* words — what exists, what it means, and who each thing was meant for when
someone specific was meant. Heirs are named on this side of the seam only when the owner chooses to
name them, and never as a group with a process. The fairness process for whatever the owner did not
specifically direct is a separate job, done later, in FairPlay — which the heirs run together,
with the trustee outside the app handling the high-value bucket and the financial balance.

## Purpose

Wills and trusts are written about money. They are rarely written about things.

An estate plan will name a successor trustee, divide percentages, and dispose of
accounts and real property in careful language — and then hand the family a
house full of objects with no list at all. That gap is where estates go wrong.
Research on inheritance conflict puts personal items at
**five times more likely to cause family conflict than money**
([Allianz Life, via Santaella Law](https://www.santaellalaw.com/blog/2026/february/how-to-prevent-family-disputes-over-sentimental-/)).
One estate firm reports that **more than half of the lawsuits they see concern
items making up less than 10% of the estate**
([Kavesh, Minor & Otis](https://www.kaveshlaw.com/blog/preventing-inheritance-fights-over-sentimental-stuff-the-law-firm-of-kavesh-minor-amp-otis-inc-.cfm)).
Tangible personal property ranks among the top three causes of conflict in
estate administration
([Wealth Management, Sandra Glazier](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/estate-planning/dividing-the-stuff-shouldn-t-be-an-afterthought)).

Reindeer: Registry closes that gap. It is the itemized personal-property
record that the will assumes exists and almost never does. One person — usually
the owner, sometimes a trust officer or family member helping them — walks
through the house and produces a written, printable inventory of what is there,
what it is, what it means, and who it is meant for when someone specific is
meant.

It is a documentation tool. It does not divide anything and it does not decide
anything. That is deliberate, and it is the reason the record holds up.

## Process

**Two ways to capture, because two situations are genuinely different.**

*One thing at a time.* Photograph an object and answer short questions in plain
language: what it is, who made it, the story behind it, which room, what kind of
thing, and who it is for. Eight steps, one screen each, large type, large
buttons. The story field is the one most families later say mattered most — it
is the only place the reason an object matters gets written down while the person
who knows it is still able to tell it.

*A lot of things at once.* Take a video walkthrough of a room, or select several
photographs. The app pulls still frames from the recording, identifies candidate
objects, and presents them for confirmation. A room that would take an afternoon
item by item takes a few minutes, and the owner reviews rather than types.

**Recipients are optional and never invented.** If there is a specific person in
mind, name them. If there is not, the field stays empty. The app will not guess,
will not distribute, and will not pressure a decision. An unassigned item is a
complete record, not an incomplete one.

**Values are not fabricated.** The app does not print an estimate it cannot
stand behind. It flags items that look like they deserve a professional opinion
— marked plainly as *worth a look later* — and leaves the number blank until a
person or an appraiser supplies one. A blank is honest. A made-up figure is a
liability, and appraisal of significant items is properly a professional's job
([JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/distributions-of-a-decedent-s-personal-3425075/)).

**Duplicates are caught, not merged.** Walking the same shelf twice, or
recording a room and then photographing one object on the shelf, will produce
two records of one thing. The app groups the likely pairs and shows them side by
side with the reason it thinks they match. A person decides which record
survives. Nothing is ever deleted automatically.

**Then it prints.** Every item is printable, and the printed output is the point
of the exercise. The document comes out in two parts:

- **Schedule A** — the items with a named recipient. This is the operative
  section, formatted as a memorandum of tangible personal property, with
  signature and witness blocks.
- **Schedule B** — everything else. Inventory only, no recipient column. This is
  the part that answers "what was in the house," which is the question the
  trustee actually has to answer first.

## Results

- **An itemized personal-property record** that exists before it is needed,
  written in the owner's own words while they can still supply them.
- **A signable memorandum.** Roughly **40 states** permit a will to refer to a
  separate writing disposing of tangible personal property
  ([Nolo](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/using-personal-property-memorandum-with-your-will.html)),
  under the Uniform Probate Code's separate-writing provision
  ([UPC §2-513](http://njwills.blogspot.com/2015/03/section-2-513-separate-writing.html))
  and state analogues such as
  [Fla. Stat. §732.515](https://csgfirm.com/memorandum-as-to-disposition-of-tangible-personal-property-attached-to-a-florida-will/).
  The app explains, in plain language, that **current law generally requires a
  printed and signed document** — electronic-signature statutes such as ESIGN
  ([NTIA](https://www.ntia.gov/files/ntia/publications/esignfinal.pdf)) and UETA
  ([ACTEC](https://actecfoundation.org/podcasts/uniform-electronic-transactions-act-ueta/))
  carve out wills and testamentary instruments, and electronic wills where
  permitted have their own separate requirements
  ([Fla. Stat. §732.522](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&Search_String=&URL=0700-0799/0732/Sections/0732.522.html)).
  So the app tells the user that, has them proceed, and then lets them
  **photograph or scan the signed document** and send it to the trustee.
- **Professional confirmation of the signing.** Where the app is handed to the
  **trustee or attorney** to complete (your legal documents may name the trustee
  *personal representative* or *executor*; either way, this app calls them
  *trustee*), that professional can confirm the document was properly signed. The signing is
  attested by a person, not asserted by software.
- **A handoff that survives.** The record exports in a documented format that
  Reindeer: FairPlay imports directly, so nothing is retyped and no meaning
  is lost between the two apps.

## Why this is better

**Practically.** The alternatives are a spreadsheet, a phone full of unlabeled
photographs, or nothing. A spreadsheet has no photographs and no story field, so
a year later nobody knows which of the three blue vases the row refers to. A
phone full of photographs has no structure. Nothing is the usual choice, and
nothing is what produces the lawsuits. This app is built for the person actually
doing the work — often someone in their seventies or eighties — with large type,
large targets, no jargon, one question per screen, and a confirmation before
anything irreversible. The video lane exists because the honest obstacle to
inventorying a house is not the interface, it is the sheer number of objects.

**Legally.** Three design choices matter more than any feature:

1. **It produces a printed, signable instrument**, aligned with the
   separate-writing statutes that most states have adopted, rather than assuming
   a database row is legally operative. It tells the user why the paper is
   needed instead of leaving them to find out later.
2. **It does not fabricate values.** A record that leaves a value blank and
   flags the item for appraisal is defensible. A record populated with
   software-generated estimates invites a challenge to every figure in it, and
   the specific bequests it supports are the ones that
   [abate last](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/document/statutes/1995/863.21) —
   they are worth documenting correctly.
3. **It keeps documentation separate from distribution.** The registry records
   intent. It never divides an estate, never scores an heir, and never runs a
   process. That separation is why the record reads as evidence of what the
   owner wanted rather than as the output of an algorithm.

**A note for trust officers.** This is documentation work that professionals are
currently asked to do with a legal pad. Handing a client this app before the
first meeting — or handing it to the family after — replaces the worst part of
the file with a printable, itemized, signed schedule.

### Honest limits

- The app does not give legal advice and is not a substitute for counsel. State
  law on separate writings varies; the memorandum must be reviewed and executed
  properly.
- It does not appraise. Flagged items still need a professional.
- The same object recorded in two **separate** walkthroughs under two different
  labels can still survive as two records. Frame grouping only works within one
  recording. The duplicate sweep is the backstop, and it only ever proposes —
  a person always decides.
- Certain categories carry their own transfer law that no inventory satisfies on
  its own. Firearms are the clearest case
  ([27 CFR §479.90a](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/27/479.90a),
  [ATF guidance on NFA firearms in estates](https://www.atf.gov/media/25196/download)).
- The registry has no sign-in of its own; it is a single-operator tool. Shared,
  authenticated access is FairPlay's job.
